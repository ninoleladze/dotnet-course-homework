PROMPT:
Table `product_title(id, title, product_category_id)` can have several rows in
`product` (each with its own manufacturer_id) sharing the same product_title_id.
Using `product`, `order_details` and `manufacturer(id, name)`, find the leading
manufacturer per product title, i.e. the manufacturer whose products (under
that title) sold the highest total product_amount. If the title was never
sold (or has no products at all), the manufacturer should be NULL. Sort
ascending by title id. Columns: product_id | title | manufacturer_id | manufacturer
(product_id here is actually the product_title id). Use a correlated subquery.

RESPONSE:
A single correlated scalar subquery can return the winning manufacturer_id for
each title: group the title's products by manufacturer, order by total quantity
sold descending (tie-broken by the lowest manufacturer id), and take the first
row. A LEFT JOIN back to `manufacturer` on that id naturally produces NULL when
the subquery itself returns NULL (no sales at all):

SELECT
    pt.id AS product_id,
    pt.title AS title,
    m.id AS manufacturer_id,
    m.name AS manufacturer
FROM product_title pt
LEFT JOIN manufacturer m ON m.id = (
    SELECT p.manufacturer_id
    FROM product p
    JOIN order_details od ON od.product_id = p.id
    WHERE p.product_title_id = pt.id
    GROUP BY p.manufacturer_id
    ORDER BY SUM(od.product_amount) DESC, p.manufacturer_id ASC
    LIMIT 1
)
ORDER BY pt.id ASC;

FOLLOW-UP PROMPT:
One of my titles ("Vodka") has two products that both sold exactly 18 units
total, from different manufacturers. Which one wins, and is that deterministic?

RESPONSE:
Yes, it's deterministic because of the second ORDER BY key: when the summed
quantity ties, `p.manufacturer_id ASC` breaks the tie by picking the lower
manufacturer id, and LIMIT 1 keeps only that top row. Without that second sort
key, SQLite would not guarantee a stable choice between tied groups.
